# Chapter 1: Foundations — AI for Inclusive Cities

## Core Idea
AI is a set of pattern-recognition tools that can identify and eliminate accessibility barriers in both physical and digital environments — but it only delivers inclusion when built on existing city resources and driven by stakeholder input, not deployed as a standalone magic box.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Categories of AI Applications**: the playbook's primary way to classify public-sector AI.
  - **Anticipatory (predictive)**: predicts future needs based on historical data. Public-sector uses: traffic patterns, energy consumption, healthcare staffing. Can take inclusion goals into account when predicting patterns and outcomes.
  - **Generative**: creates new content (text, images, music) resembling human creativity. Uses: chatbots, legislative text generation, urban design renderings. With proactive guidance, can support inclusive conversations and communities.
  - **Decisive**: automates complex decision-making and provides recommendations based on patterns. Uses: assessing program effectiveness, efficient resource allocation, data-driven infrastructure decisions. Care must be taken to ensure equitable, inclusive decisions.
  - When to use: categorize any proposed municipal AI application into one of these three before assessing it — the category sets the risk profile and the inclusion levers available.
- **AI Capabilities for Inclusion**: seven capability types to inspire and scope AI use cases.
  - **Detection & Classification** — identify/categorize/translate images, numbers, sound. Examples: asset inventories (digital maps of the urban landscape), sign language interpretation.
  - **Forecasting & Prediction** — estimate the next value in a series. Examples: transit asset failure prediction, route obstruction prediction, disaster risk alerting.
  - **Clustering** — group similar objects for efficient planning (economies of scale).
  - **Optimization** — output that optimizes for a set of outcomes. Examples: prioritizing infrastructure by cost/inclusivity/equity, home automation for mobility independence.
  - **Anomaly Detection** — flag deviations from a pattern. Examples: real-time wheelchair-accessible transit updates, fall/accident detection with emergency alerts.
  - **Decision-making** — automate or suggest decisions. Examples: assistive navigation for visually impaired people, adaptive traffic signals adjusting to pedestrian pace.
  - **Generation** — create new content. Examples: chatbots helping residents navigate services in natural language.

## Key Concepts
- **Artificial intelligence**: information-processing technologies that integrate models and algorithms to learn and perform cognitive tasks, leading to prediction and decision-making (UNESCO definition).
- **Accessibility**: an international human right and precondition for inclusion — the extent to which products, systems, services, and environments can be used by people with the widest range of characteristics.
- **Universal Design**: design that creates products, environments, and services usable and inclusive for all people regardless of age, ability, or social status.
- **Digital inclusion**: ensuring all individuals have equal access to, and ability to use, digital technologies and the internet.
- **Algorithmic bias**: unfair or discriminatory outcomes when algorithms favor or disadvantage certain groups due to training data, technical design, or policies.
- **Digital redlining**: algorithms systematically excluding groups based on income, location, or other factors — akin to historical housing redlining.
- **Assistive technology**: hardware or software that increases accessibility for an individual with mental or physical disabilities.

## Mental Models
- **Think of AI as an amalgam, not a box**: AI builds upon existing city databases, physical/digital assets, collective knowledge, and community engagement processes. Audit what the city already has before imagining a new tool.
- **"Garbage in, garbage out"**: if training data is not diverse and representative, AI reproduces existing stereotypes and biases. Data quality is outcome quality.
- **AI speeds up analysis, it doesn't create new abilities**: AI makes analyzing data and decision-making faster without needing people to do the work — don't expect capabilities the data cannot support.
- **Use AI categories as a scoping lens**: classify a proposed tool as anticipatory, generative, or decisive first, then ask what inclusion can be built into each.

## Anti-patterns
- **Overestimating AI**: assuming AI is more powerful than it is, or that it creates abilities out of nowhere. It accelerates analysis — the data and process still matter.
- **Ignoring data rights**: building AI on private or sensitive information without protecting it harms the very people the technology is meant to help. Plan data protection from the start.
- **Prescribing AI to every problem**: not every city issue needs AI; existing technology may be improvable without it, or technology may not be the right solution at all.

## Worked Example
Classifying three candidate projects for an inclusive city using the Three Categories lens:

1. **Anticipatory — sidewalk obstruction prediction**: historical street condition data feeds a model that predicts which sidewalks are most likely to become obstructed or fail. Inclusion lever: weight predictions so routes used by people with mobility aids and older residents are prioritized.
2. **Decisive — infrastructure prioritization**: a system recommends which accessibility repair projects to fund first, optimizing cost, inclusivity, and equity. Inclusion lever: audit the fairness metrics — the recommendation must not silently deprioritize marginalized neighborhoods.
3. **Generative — accessibility chatbot**: a natural-language assistant helps residents navigate city services and translates information across languages. Inclusion lever: test with screen readers and users with cognitive disabilities before launch.

Same capability set (forecasting, decision, generation) — but the risk and the inclusion design work differ per category.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify every AI application as anticipatory, generative, or decisive before assessing it — the category determines risk and inclusion levers.
2. Use the seven capability types to brainstorm concrete use cases, then validate them against real accessibility barriers.
3. AI is only as good as its data — diverse, representative training data is a non-negotiable inclusion requirement.
4. Always pair AI capability with data protection and privacy safeguards; the intended beneficiaries must not be harmed.
5. Start from existing city assets and community knowledge, not from the technology.
