# Patterns — AI Playbook for Inclusive and Accessible Cities

## Ten Step Framework
**When to use**: starting any AI-for-accessibility project in a city or large organization.
**How**: (1) engage stakeholders and define values; (2) identify barriers to inclusion; (3) conduct a technology and data inventory; (4) anticipate and mitigate risks; (5) select a technology solution; (6) develop a prototype or pilot; (7) review initial results and adjust; (8) scale; (9) support through communication and training; (10) monitor and evaluate.
**Trade-offs**: thorough and people-first but coordination-heavy; participatory steps fail without dedicated resourcing.

## Barrier Categorization
**When to use**: during the Step 2 needs assessment, to ensure no barrier class is missed.
**How**: check each category — historic infrastructure; shortcomings in planning; information and communication barriers; transportation gaps; emergency preparedness; inefficient resource distribution.
**Trade-offs**: systematic coverage vs. one more checklist; pair with lived-experience feedback or it becomes abstract.

## Asset Inventory
**When to use**: at project outset, before creating anything new.
**How**: catalog existing policies and public commitments, roadmaps and accessibility reviews, grants and their metrics, governing bodies and resident councils, and advocacy-dedicated staff; then gather targeted feedback via interviews, focus groups, workshops, and innovation work groups.
**Trade-offs**: front-loads discovery work but prevents duplicating existing efforts and grounds the project in city commitments.

## Data Inventory
**When to use**: once barriers are known, before selecting any AI tool (Step 3).
**How**: ask what technology exists for the issue, how it performs against values, what can be repurposed or expanded, and who the teams/vendors are; then map dataset types — automated, surveillance, user-generated, crowdsourced, administrative, survey, social media, documents. Reuse existing city systems or data-sharing agreements before collecting new data.
**Trade-offs**: avoids waste and "garbage in, garbage out", but existing data is often not cleaned or stored for AI use.

## Pre-mortem
**When to use**: before committing resources to any technology decision.
**How**: a facilitator asks reviewers to pretend the project has already failed and enumerate why, drawing on personal experience and technical knowledge; include experts with lived experience early.
**Trade-offs**: cheap and fast, but only as good as the diversity of the reviewers assembled.

## AI Register
**When to use**: whenever a city operates AI, to make the transparency principle operational.
**How**: publish a plain-language inventory of every AI tool — what it does, the data it uses or creates, how people oversee it, how risks are handled. Pair with "open by default": share documents and code unless privacy says otherwise.
**Trade-offs**: significant upkeep; the payoff is public trust and inspectability.

## Bug Bashing
**When to use**: evaluating a prototype/pilot with first-time users (Step 7).
**How**: ask people who have never used the technology to try it and record every issue — broken parts, confusing actions, design feedback — as bugs for the team to fix.
**Trade-offs**: surfaces real friction cheaply; must include intended users (older persons, people with disabilities), not just internal staff.

## Red Teaming
**When to use**: stress-testing an inclusive-AI system before scale.
**How**: testers intentionally try to produce errors and harmful outcomes, specifically looking for results that would hinder inclusivity and accessibility.
**Trade-offs**: uncovers failure modes testing rarely finds; requires skilled adversarial testers and community participation.

## Accessibility Safari (Ground-Truth Validation)
**When to use**: validating AI output against physical reality before trusting it at scale.
**How**: have teams physically measure real-world conditions (e.g. sidewalk widths) in selected locations and compare them against the AI dataset; involve students and community stakeholders as validators.
**Trade-offs**: high-quality ground truth and awareness-raising, but labor-intensive and limited to sampled locations.

## Multi-Tiered Monitoring
**When to use**: for the entire project life cycle, not just at the end (Step 10).
**How**: resource a dedicated project manager; run weekly check-ins with the core team on deliverables and KPIs, and quarterly/annual assessments with broader community stakeholders (users, disability community, academic partners).
**Trade-offs**: continuous course correction vs. ongoing cost; without resourcing, participatory monitoring collapses.

## Lived-Experience Hiring & Compensation
**When to use**: whenever community stakeholders contribute to a project.
**How**: compensate participants for their expertise and hire community members onto the team (e.g. Spectrum Intelligence's 13,000-point data labeling); budget for this in funding plans and grants.
**Trade-offs**: extra budget and coordination; pays off in data quality, equity, and trust.

## Feasibility Gate for AI Selection
**When to use**: at every candidate technology decision (Step 5).
**How**: verify the right data, skills, and budget exist to support a small test; check risks to privacy, civil rights, and civil liberties; confirm AI outperforms improving existing technology — otherwise don't use AI.
**Trade-offs**: rejects some exciting AI ideas, but prevents expensive, rights-risky failures.

## Responsible AI Policy Suite
**When to use**: before deploying public AI (Appendix C).
**How**: put in place six policies — data privacy and consent; bias mitigation; transparency and accountability; accessibility and inclusivity; copyright; civil rights protection.
**Trade-offs**: heavy compliance overhead; essential for lawful, ethical, inclusive deployment and defensible against challenge.
