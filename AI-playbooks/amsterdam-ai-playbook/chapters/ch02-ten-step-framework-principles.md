# Chapter 2: The Ten Step Framework & Core Principles

## Core Idea
The Ten Step Framework is the playbook's central method for developing inclusive AI: a repeatable sequence from stakeholder values to monitoring and evaluation, grounded in five ethical principles and the disability rights maxim "nothing about us without us."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ten Step Framework for developing inclusive AI**: the master process for any city AI-for-inclusion project.
  1. **Engage Stakeholders and Define Values** — engage advocacy groups, community organizations, and individuals; agree on values; proactively ask about risks to privacy, human rights, and civil liberties.
  2. **Identify Barriers to Inclusion** — with stakeholders, identify specific accessibility challenges for older persons and people with disabilities.
  3. **Conduct a Technology and Data Inventory** — review existing technology and datasets available for tool development and performance monitoring.
  4. **Anticipate and Mitigate Risks** — identify ethical risks (accessibility, fairness, bias, data privacy, misuse) before committing to new technology.
  5. **Select a Technology Solution** — engage technology experts; estimate feasibility; define scope of work.
  6. **Develop a Prototype or Pilot** — build a smaller version or pilot in one area/for one issue.
  7. **Review Initial Results and Adjust** — gather feedback from users and stakeholders; identify strengths and improvements.
  8. **Scale** — expand to larger areas and services; develop a monitoring and evaluation framework.
  9. **Support Through Communication and Training** — raise awareness and train users among residents, officials, and service providers.
  10. **Monitor and Evaluate** — evaluate impact on accessibility; build in ongoing community feedback; share findings responsibly.
  - When to use: at project inception of any AI-for-accessibility initiative; revisit as a checklist at each stage.
  - How: run steps sequentially, but loop back to stakeholders (Steps 1–2, 4, 7) at every phase.
- **Five Ethical Principles for AI for Inclusive Urban Development**: agreed in dialogue with community stakeholders and international best practice.
  1. **Accessibility** — new technology is easy for everyone to use; provide training so people with disabilities and older citizens can use AI tools independently.
  2. **Transparency and Accountability** — explain how technology works in formats understandable to non-technical audiences, especially when AI replaces human decision-makers or affects access to services.
  3. **User-Centered Design** — develop solutions with strong focus on end users; engage people with disabilities in design and testing; include people with lived experience on the team.
  4. **Diversity and Representation** — training inputs must be diverse and representative; test and correct bias in source material, or add guardrails against bias in outcomes.
  5. **Scalability and Sustainability** — design for longevity; identify funding streams beyond initial investment; weigh natural-resource impacts (energy and water) against benefits.

## Key Concepts
- **Nothing about us without us**: the disability rights movement's motto — the guiding test for whether stakeholders were genuinely included.
- **Tada Manifesto**: Amsterdam's six agreed principles for work as a digital city — inclusivity, control, tailored to the people, legitimacy, openness, transparency.
- **Ethical Leaflet**: Amsterdam's tool for defining moral motivations and implications before starting a project, built on questions like "Why are we doing this?" and "For whom?"
- **Customize This Framework**: before deploying, consider local context (geography, demographics, unique features), political priorities, and how leaders and community align on specific needs.
- **Audience roles**: City Innovators (anticipate opportunities/challenges for strategic planning), Community Stakeholders (advocate for end-user involvement), Technology Teams (execute the step-by-step build and deployment).

## Mental Models
- **Stakeholders first, technology later**: values and barrier identification (Steps 1–2) must precede any talk of solutions — the framework is people-first by construction.
- **Think of each principle as a design constraint**: run every decision against the five principles (accessibility, transparency, user-centeredness, diversity, sustainability) as a cheap acceptance test.
- **Localize before you execute**: the framework is a skeleton; flesh it out with the city's geography, demographics, political priorities, and existing commitments.

## Anti-patterns
- **Skipping Step 1**: jumping to technology selection without stakeholder-defined values produces tools nobody asked for and risks that were never surfaced.
- **Treating the framework as one-way linear**: failing to loop back to stakeholders at Steps 4 and 7 ignores the feedback the process is designed to collect.
- **Ignoring resource impacts**: building an AI tool without planning funding longevity or accounting for energy/water use — "scalability and sustainability" is a principle, not an afterthought.
- **Compensating no one**: expecting marginalized people to contribute lived experience for free undermines the equity the framework claims.

## Worked Example
Using the Ethical Leaflet at project start (as Amsterdam did):

- "Why are we doing this?" — Residents with mobility aids report sidewalks they cannot use; the city cannot survey 100,000 streets manually.
- "For whom?" — Wheelchair users, older residents using walkers, caregivers with strollers, people with visual impairments.
- "What does inclusion demand here?" — The 2D maps cannot capture obstacles; three-dimensional data is required.
- "Who decides?" — Advocacy groups and users with lived experience co-create the priorities; their input and the answers are published in line with the Tada value of transparency.

Running this before any technology talk yields a scoped, principled project brief.

## Key Takeaways
1. Follow the Ten Step Framework in order but return to stakeholders at every phase — it is a loop, not a line.
2. Validate every decision against the five ethical principles; they act as an early acceptance test.
3. Apply "nothing about us without us" as the standing question: were the affected community actually in the room?
4. Use tools like the Ethical Leaflet and Tada Manifesto to make values explicit and public before committing resources.
5. Localize the framework to each city's context — no two deployments should look identical.
