# Chapter 6: Steps 8–10 — Scale, Communicate, Monitor

## Core Idea
The final steps take a proven tool city-wide while protecting quality and inclusivity: scale deliberately (Step 8), communicate and train so people can actually use it (Step 9), and run dedicated, resourced monitoring and evaluation that lasts the whole project life cycle (Step 10).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Scale with Guardrails (Step 8)**:
  - When to use: after the pilot is evaluated and adjusted.
  - How: expand to larger areas or more services, while verifying the technology can scale without losing quality or inclusivity. Reuse validated data and models in adjacent pilots; keep running the full framework (stakeholder engagement, workshops, risk management) in each new application.
- **Communication & Training (Step 9)**:
  - When to use: from the moment a tool nears deployment.
  - How: market the tool to residents, city leaders, and service providers to build internal support, attract funding, and inspire other departments. Provide training and written materials to people who will use and maintain the tools, and include community stakeholders in that training so they can engage with future AI projects.
- **Multi-Tiered Monitoring (Step 10)**:
  - When to use: for the entire project life cycle, not just launch.
  - How: resource a dedicated project manager empowered to define KPIs and check regularly whether the AI is improving accessibility and inclusion. Run frequent (weekly) check-ins with the core team on deliverables and KPIs, and periodic (quarterly/annual) assessments with the broader community — users, disability community, academic partners — to align expectations and capture real-world application.

## Key Concepts
- **KPI-driven adjustment**: tracking key performance indicators weekly so course corrections happen immediately rather than at project end.
- **Open source as communication**: releasing code publicly demonstrates transparency and invites reuse by other cities.
- **Adjacent pilots**: reusing validated assets (e.g. sidewalk-width data) in new use cases like wheelchair route planning.
- **Participatory failure mode**: participatory approaches often fail when the added coordination and engagement they require is not resourced — hence the dedicated project manager.
- **Sustainable funding**: scaling requires ongoing funding streams and weighing resource costs against benefits, per the sustainability principle.

## Mental Models
- **Think of scaling as a second validation, not a victory lap**: each new area or service re-tests the technology; quality and inclusion must be re-verified at every expansion.
- **Treat communication as a scaling force**: awareness drives adoption, funding, and cross-department imitation — the return on Step 9 is multiplied by Step 8.
- **Monitoring is a lifecycle posture, not a deadline**: review continuously (weekly with the team) and periodically (quarterly/annual with the community) — never just at the end.
- **Whoever does the work must be resourced**: participation and coordination cost money and staff; under-resourcing them is the classic way inclusive projects stall.

## Anti-patterns
- **Scaling unproven quality**: expanding before verifying the technology holds up at larger scope.
- **Building tools nobody knows about**: deploying without marketing or training — residents, officials, and service providers cannot use what they don't understand.
- **One-shot evaluation**: assessing impact only at the end; the playbook requires monitoring throughout the life cycle with a dedicated PM.
- **Unfunded participation**: adding stakeholder engagement without resourcing coordination — a leading cause of participatory project failure.
- **Hoarding data**: not sharing results and open code with colleagues, civil society, and other cities, missing the network effects of transparency.

## Worked Example
Amsterdam's multi-tiered monitoring model (Step 10):

1. **Weekly — core team**: a dedicated Project Manager ran weekly meetings tracking deliverables and KPIs, enabling immediate adjustments and responsive development. These sessions also gave technical teams and partners a platform for strategic discussion and collaborative problem-solving.
2. **Quarterly/annual — broader community**: larger gatherings with users, members of the disability community, and academic partners aligned expectations, gathered diverse feedback, and tested the real-world application of the emerging tools.
3. **Lifecycle posture**: monitoring was resourced and sustained throughout the project, not bolted on at the end.

Meanwhile, communication (Step 9) ran in parallel: 9 blog posts, 3 academic publications, interviews, community workshop findings, and released open-source code — supporting a wider strategy to analyze existing disability-inclusion AI, engage the community through multiple channels, and build an international partner network.

## Key Takeaways
1. Scale only what has proven quality and inclusivity in pilot — guardrails come with expansion.
2. Pair every deployment with communication and training; adoption and funding follow awareness.
3. Resource a dedicated project manager for monitoring; KPIs checked weekly drive immediate adjustments.
4. Add periodic community-level assessments (quarterly/annual) to keep the project aligned with real-world impact.
5. Share data, findings, and open code — transparency multiplies impact across other cities and departments.
